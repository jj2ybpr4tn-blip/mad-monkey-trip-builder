When to go

Best months: Jan, Nov and Dec. Expect rain or rough seas in Mar, Apr, Sep and Oct.

Festivals and closures to plan around

  • Loy Krathong + Yi Peng lanterns Mon 23 Nov to Thu 26 Nov

    Thousands of lanterns over Chiang Mai. Unforgettable, and beds in the north sell out weeks ahead. Lock in Chiang Mai and Pai beds early. Dates follow the full moon, so check the official ones closer to the time.

  • Christmas + New Year peak Sun 20 Dec to Tue 5 Jan

    The busiest and dearest weeks of the year across the region. Popular hostels, island boats and flights sell out. Book early and avoid one-night stops. It is worth paying for a bed you have held.

  • Tet (Vietnamese New Year) 4–10 Feb, Mon 1 Feb to Thu 11 Feb

    Vietnam's biggest holiday. Many shops, restaurants and tour operators close for days, trains and buses sell out a week either side, and fares jump. Book every bus, train and flight early, expect some closures, and base yourself on the coast. Or travel outside it.

  • Chinese New Year crowds Thu 4 Feb to Sun 14 Feb

    Big regional travel peak. Flights, hotels and popular day tours fill up and cost more, especially Bangkok, Phuket and Siem Reap. Book transport and your Angkor or island trips early.

  • Songkran water festival Tue 13 Apr to Thu 15 Apr

    Thailand's New Year is a nationwide water fight. It is a brilliant few days. Everyone gets soaked, many shops close, and transport is packed. Keep your phone in a dry bag, book buses early, and treat Chiang Mai as the main event.

  • Khmer New Year Tue 13 Apr to Fri 16 Apr

    Cambodians travel home. Phnom Penh goes quiet, buses to Siem Reap and the coast fill, and the water-throwing starts. Book buses and boats early. Siem Reap is lively, Phnom Penh is sleepy.

  • Pi Mai (Lao New Year) Wed 14 Apr to Fri 16 Apr

    Water festival and processions. Luang Prabang is at its best and busiest, and many businesses close. Book Luang Prabang beds and the Kuang Si trip ahead.

Lunar and government holiday dates can move. Checked Oct 2026.

Know before you go

Riding a scooter? Read this first.
  • Most travel insurers will only pay out if you hold the right licence for the bike you were riding. Read your policy wording, and ask them in writing.
  • Wear a proper helmet every ride, closed shoes and long trousers. Don't ride tired, drunk or in the dark.
  • Photograph the bike on all sides before you ride, and never hand over your passport as a deposit.
  • If you've never ridden, the road is not the place to learn. Take a lesson, or join a guided ride with a driver.

Thailand

Money

  • Thailand uses baht (THB). Cash is king at markets, street food stalls and for tuk-tuks. Cards are fine in malls, 7-Elevens and bigger restaurants.
  • Foreign cards pay a fixed fee on every ATM withdrawal (around 220 to 250 baht, and AEON machines are usually cheaper), so take out the most the machine allows, less often.

Getting online

  • A tourist eSIM is the easiest way. Set it up before you land and you're online as you walk out of the airport.
  • Local SIMs (AIS, True, dtac) are sold at airports and 7-Elevens. Take your passport.

Getting around

  • Grab and Bolt work in Bangkok, Chiang Mai and Phuket and save you haggling. In a taxi, insist on the meter.
  • In Bangkok the BTS Skytrain and MRT beat the traffic every time. In Chiang Mai the red songthaew trucks are a cheap shared ride.

Scooters + licences

  • To ride legally you need a motorcycle licence from home plus an International Driving Permit with the motorcycle category (a car-only permit doesn't cover a scooter). Many shops will still rent without checking, which is no protection if something goes wrong.
  • Police check tourists at hotspots, and helmets are enforced. Pai's roads and Phuket's hills cause a lot of accidents every year.

Staying safe

  • Vapes and e-cigarettes are illegal in Thailand. Leave yours at home.
  • Drugs carry some of the harshest penalties in the region. Don't.

Manners

  • Cover shoulders and knees at temples and the Grand Palace, and take your shoes off before going inside.
  • Never point your feet at a Buddha statue or a person, and don't touch anyone's head.

Health

  • Don't drink the tap water. Refill at your hostel or buy bottles.
  • Mosquitoes carry dengue. Use repellent at dawn and dusk.

Laos

Money

  • Laos uses the kip (LAK), and the notes are huge. Bring clean US dollars or Thai baht as a backup.
  • ATMs are in the main towns, with low limits and fees. Take out what you need before heading to small places.

Getting online

  • Tourist eSIMs work in the main towns, and coverage thins out in the mountains.
  • Local SIMs (Lao Telecom, Unitel) are sold with a passport. Download maps offline.

Getting around

  • The Laos-China railway makes Luang Prabang to Vang Vieng and Vientiane quick and easy. Book tickets ahead, and allow time for the station transfers.
  • The slow boat from Thailand takes two days with a night in Pakbeng. Take food, a cushion and a warm layer.

Scooters + licences

  • Foreigners are expected to carry a valid licence plus an International Driving Permit, and an accident without one can become very expensive.
  • Roads are narrow and often unlit, with animals on them. Avoid riding after dark.

Staying safe

  • Vapes are banned in Laos. Leave yours at home.
  • Unexploded bombs from the war still lie in parts of the country. Stay on marked paths and never touch anything metal in the countryside.

Manners

  • Dress modestly at temples, and never point your feet at a Buddha statue.
  • At the dawn alms giving in Luang Prabang, watch quietly from a respectful distance and don't buy from the sellers who push you. Don't use flash.

Health

  • Drink only bottled or filtered water.
  • Malaria risk exists in rural areas, and dengue is widespread. Ask a travel clinic about both.

Vietnam

Money

  • Vietnam uses the dong (VND), and the numbers are big. Take care with the zeros on notes.
  • ATMs are everywhere in cities. Limits per withdrawal are low, so you pay a fee more often.

Getting online

  • A tourist eSIM is the easiest. Local SIMs (Viettel, Vinaphone) are cheap with a passport.
  • Grab works in Hanoi and Saigon for rides, food and parcels, so it is useful for much more than taxis.

Getting around

  • Use Grab for taxis and bikes. It is cheaper and removes the meter arguments.
  • Crossing the road: walk at a steady pace, don't stop or run, and the traffic flows round you.

Scooters + licences

  • Vietnam wants a valid motorcycle licence plus an International Driving Permit under the 1968 convention with the motorcycle category (A or A1). Many US, Canadian and Australian permits are issued under the older 1949 convention and may not count.
  • Riding without the right licence risks a fine and the bike being impounded, and it is likely to affect your insurance.

Staying safe

  • Vapes and e-cigarettes are banned in Vietnam, and tourists have been fined for vaping.
  • Bags and phones are snatched from motorbikes. Carry your bag away from the road, and don't hold your phone out near traffic.

Manners

  • Cover shoulders and knees at pagodas and temples.
  • Take your shoes off when you enter a home, and point your feet away from the family altar.

Health

  • Drink bottled or filtered water, and brush your teeth with it.
  • Air quality in Hanoi can be poor in winter. Check the AQI if you have asthma.

Cambodia

Money

  • Cambodia runs on US dollars alongside the riel. ATMs dispense dollars, and small change comes back in riel (4,000 riel is about $1).
  • Bring crisp, undamaged notes. Torn or marked dollars are often refused.

Getting online

  • Local SIMs (Cellcard, Smart, Metfone) are cheap and sold at the airport with your passport. A tourist eSIM is easier.
  • Coverage is good in towns and patchy on the islands.

Getting around

  • PassApp and Grab work in Phnom Penh and Siem Reap. Tuk-tuks are great for temple days: agree a day rate with the driver.
  • Buses between Phnom Penh, Siem Reap, Kampot and the coast are cheap and frequent. Book the day before in busy periods.

Scooters + licences

  • Check the rules and the rental company's insurance before you ride. Riding without a valid licence can void your insurance and leave you liable.
  • Roads outside the cities are rough and shared with animals and unlit trucks. Avoid riding at night.

Staying safe

  • Vapes are banned in Cambodia. Leave yours at home.
  • Bag-snatching from passing motorbikes is common in Phnom Penh. Carry your bag away from the road, and keep your phone away from the kerb.

Manners

  • Cover your shoulders and knees at Angkor and at every pagoda. Temples can turn you away.
  • Don't touch monks (women), don't point your feet at Buddha images, and don't touch heads.

Health

  • Drink bottled or filtered water.
  • Dengue is common. Use repellent and wear light long sleeves at dusk.
